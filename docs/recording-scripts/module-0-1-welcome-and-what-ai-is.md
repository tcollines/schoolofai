## m1-l2 | What AI is, and what it isn't | target 7 min

**Learning objective:** The learner can state what AI is in one sentence and name three common mistakes about it.

**Slides to prepare:**
1. Title: "What AI is, and what it isn't".
2. Definition: "AI is a computer doing a job that normally needs human thinking." Under it: understanding speech, making decisions, translating languages.
3. Three examples in three boxes: Google Maps, Google Translate, your bank app, each with "Before: ..." underneath.
4. "Three things AI is not": not a robot; not new; not a person thinking.
5. "Is it thinking?" A simple two-line slide: "It finds patterns in data. It is not a human mind."
6. "Say it in one sentence": a blank line for the learner.

**Script:**

[ON SCREEN: Slide 1, title]

In the last video, we saw that you already use AI every day. Now let us give it a clear definition.

Here is the simplest one I know.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 2, the definition]

AI is a computer doing a job that normally needs human thinking.

That is it. AI stands for artificial intelligence. Artificial means made by people, not natural. Intelligence means the ability to understand, decide and solve problems. So, artificial intelligence is a computer doing things we would normally expect a human brain to do.

What kind of things? Understanding speech. Making decisions. Translating languages.

[PAUSE]

[ON SCREEN: Slide 3, three examples]

Let us look at three examples, and for each one, ask: who did this job before?

The first is Google Maps. It picks the fastest route and guesses when you will arrive. Before, you needed someone who knew the roads. Think of a boda boda rider who has ridden around Entebbe for years. He knows which road floods and which junction is blocked at five o'clock. Maps does a version of that job on your phone.

The second is Google Translate. It turns Luganda into English, and back again. Before, you needed a human translator. Imagine you have a message in Luganda from a relative, and you want to share it with a friend who only reads English. A translation tool can give you a quick first version.

The third is your bank app. Your bank app flags a strange payment on your account. Before, you needed a clerk watching accounts, line by line.

Three different jobs. Route, translation, watching for strange payments. You may use all three in a single day without a second thought. But each one is a job that used to need a human, and now a computer does it.

[PAUSE]

[ON SCREEN: Slide 3 stays; highlight "Before" lines]

Notice something here. In all three, the AI is doing one narrow job. It is not running your life. It is helping with a task.

Now, I want to be careful about this word, AI. It gets used in many ways, and some of those ways confuse people. So let us talk about three common mistakes.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 4, three things AI is not]

Mistake number one: AI is a robot.

Many people picture a metal person with a face and arms. That comes from films. But most AI has no body at all. Most AI is just software. It lives on your phone, or in a data centre. A data centre is a building full of powerful computers that are connected to the internet. There is no metal body. When you use Google Maps, you are not talking to a robot. You are using a program.

[PAUSE]

Mistake number two: AI is new.

You might think AI was invented in the last couple of years. It was not. The name dates from the 1950s. People have been working on it for a very long time. That is why spam filters and map apps have been around for years.

So what is new? One special kind of AI is new. It is called generative AI, and it can create text, pictures and more. We will meet it properly in a few videos. For now, just remember: AI is old. One kind of AI is new.

[PAUSE]

Mistake number three: AI is a person thinking.

This is the hardest one, because the results can look so much like thinking. When a chatbot answers you in good sentences, it feels like there is someone on the other side.

But there is not a person there. AI finds patterns in data. Data is just information, such as words, numbers, pictures or sounds. The AI looks at a huge amount of it, finds the patterns, and uses them. The results can look like thinking. But it is not a human mind.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 5, is it thinking]

People often ask me, so, is it really thinking? The honest answer is this. It finds patterns, and the results can look like thinking, but it is not a person. Experts still argue about how best to describe it. For our purposes, you do not need to settle that argument. You only need to remember: it finds patterns, and it is not a human mind.

[PAUSE]

Why does this matter to you in practice?

Think of a small bakery in Entebbe. The owner uses a chatbot to help write a message to customers. If she thinks the chatbot is a wise person who knows her business, she may trust it too much. But if she knows it is a pattern-finding tool, she will read what it writes, and fix anything that is wrong, before she sends it. That small difference in understanding protects her business.

[PAUSE]

[ON SCREEN: Slide 6, say it in one sentence]

Let me put it all together. AI is a computer doing a job that normally needs human thinking, by finding patterns in data.

You can repeat that, in your own words. Try it out loud now, as if you were explaining AI to a friend at a market stall.

[PAUSE]

If you said something like, AI is software that does jobs that normally need a person, like choosing a route or translating a language, then you have it.

And remember the three mistakes. AI is not a robot. AI is not new. And AI is not a person thinking.

**Pause-and-try prompt:** Explain AI to a friend, out loud or in writing, in one or two sentences. Use one example from your own phone, such as Maps or translation.

**Recap line:** AI is a computer doing a job that normally needs human thinking, and it is not a robot, it is not new, and it is not a person.

## m1-l3 | Machine learning: from rules to examples | target 8 min

**Learning objective:** The learner can explain the difference between writing rules and learning from examples, and why more examples usually helps.

**Slides to prepare:**
1. Title: "Machine learning: from rules to examples".
2. "The old way: you write the rules". Example: "If a message says You have won, it is spam." Then: "Congratulations, you are selected."
3. "A million rules, and still miss things."
4. "The machine learning way: you show examples". 10,000 messages, each marked spam or not spam. "It finds the pattern by itself."
5. A picture of a child, many mangoes, and the word "mango".
6. "More examples, better results."
7. Definition: "Machine learning means learning from examples instead of following written rules."
8. "Try it": ripe and unripe fruit, five examples of each.

**Script:**

[ON SCREEN: Slide 1, title]

In the last video, we said that AI is a computer doing a job that normally needs human thinking. But how does a computer learn to do such a job? Nobody opens up a computer and pours knowledge in. So how does it happen?

There are two ways. An old way, and a newer way. Let us start with the old way.

[PAUSE]

[ON SCREEN: Slide 2, the old way]

The old way is that a person writes the rules.

Let us use spam as our example. Spam is unwanted messages, often trying to trick you. Imagine you are the person building a spam filter, and you must tell the computer what spam looks like. You think for a moment, and you write a rule.

If a message says, "You have won", it is spam.

Good. That works for a while. A message arrives saying, "You have won a free phone, send your details", and the computer puts it in the spam folder. You feel clever.

But then the scammers notice. They are not stupid. They change the words. Now the message says, "Congratulations, you are selected".

Your rule does not catch it. The words are different. The computer follows your rule exactly, and the rule says nothing about "selected". So the message lands in the inbox.

[PAUSE]

So you write a second rule. If a message says, "Congratulations, you are selected", it is spam. Then the scammers change the words again. And you write a third rule. And a fourth.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 3, a million rules]

You can see where this goes. You would need a million rules. And you would still miss things. Because scammers are creative, and there is always one more way to say it.

Think of scam messages that pretend to be from a mobile money service. They can say almost anything. No person could write every rule needed to catch them all. The rule-writing way is slow, and it breaks easily.

[PAUSE]

[ON SCREEN: Slide 4, the machine learning way]

So here is the newer way. This is machine learning.

Instead of writing rules, you show examples.

You take 10,000 messages. Each one is marked by a person: this one is spam, this one is not spam. Spam, not spam, spam, spam, not spam. You show all 10,000 to the computer.

And then you let the computer find the pattern by itself.

You do not tell it that "You have won" is a warning sign. You do not give it the words. It looks across all 10,000 messages and works out what spam tends to look like, and what ordinary messages tend to look like. When a new message arrives, it compares it with the patterns it found, and makes a call.

[PAUSE]

Let me say that again, because it is the most important idea in this video. In the old way, a person writes the rules. In the machine learning way, the computer learns the pattern from examples.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 7, the definition]

So here is the definition. Machine learning means learning from examples instead of following written rules.

The word machine just means the computer. The word learning means it improves from what it is shown. Machine learning is the computer learning from examples.

[PAUSE]

[ON SCREEN: Slide 5, the child and the mango]

Now, if this feels strange, think about how a child learns what a mango is.

Imagine a small child at a market stall in Entebbe. The seller holds up a mango and says, "mango". Later, another mango. "Mango". A big one, a small one, a green one, a yellow one. Each time, the child hears the word.

You never gave the child a list of rules. You did not say, a mango is a round or oval fruit, with a certain skin, with a stone inside, and the colour can vary. You just showed many mangoes.

After a while, a new mango appears, one the child has never seen. The child looks at it and says, "mango".

The child found the pattern. You showed examples, and the child found the pattern from them. That is machine learning. It is exactly the same idea.

[PAUSE]

[ON SCREEN: Slide 6, more examples, better results]

There is one more point. The more examples the computer sees, the better it usually gets.

A child who has seen three mangoes may be unsure about a very small green one. A child who has seen a hundred mangoes will say "mango" without thinking. It is the same with the spam filter. Show it 10,000 messages and it does well. Show it far more, and it usually does better.

That is why so many examples matter. Examples are data, which means the information a computer learns from: messages, photos, sounds, numbers. In machine learning, data is the teacher.

[PAUSE]

Let us compare the two ways side by side.

The rules way. A person writes every rule. It is slow. And it breaks easily when something new appears.

The machine learning way. The computer learns the pattern from examples. When something new appears, it can often cope, because it learned a pattern and not a list.

Does the machine learning way always get it right? No. It can still make mistakes. But it can handle the variety of the real world far better than a million hand-written rules.

[PAUSE]

[ON SCREEN: Slide 8, try it]

Now it is your turn. Let me explain the task.

Think of something you could sort into two groups. For example, ripe mangoes and unripe mangoes. Or good tomatoes and bad tomatoes at a market. Or fabric that is good enough to sell and fabric that is not.

Now describe five examples of each group. For ripe mangoes, you might say, soft to the touch, a sweet smell, yellow and red colour. For unripe, hard, no smell, green.

Write down five examples of each group, using your notebook or your phone. That list is exactly what a machine learning system would be shown. You have just made a tiny training set.

Pause the video and try it now.

[PAUSE]

Well done. If you did that, you have done the main job in machine learning: choosing good examples.

**Pause-and-try prompt:** Think of something you could sort into two groups, such as ripe and unripe fruit. Describe five examples of each group. That is what a machine learning system would be shown.

**Recap line:** Machine learning means the computer learns the pattern from examples instead of following rules that a person wrote, and more examples usually means better results.

**Bridge:** Next, we meet deep learning, and we see how all these terms fit together like circles inside circles.

## m1-l4 | Deep learning and the AI family | target 7 min

**Learning objective:** The learner can explain how AI, machine learning, deep learning and generative AI fit together, and what deep learning is good at.

**Slides to prepare:**
1. Title: "Deep learning and the AI family".
2. "Circles inside circles": taxi, car, vehicle drawn as nested circles.
3. The AI family as nested circles: AI, machine learning, deep learning, generative AI.
4. "Layers that build up": Layer 1 edges and lines; Layer 2 shapes; Layer 3 "that looks like an eye"; Layer 4 "that is a face".
5. "What deep learning is": "Loosely inspired by how brain cells connect. It is maths, not a brain."
6. "What it is good at": messy data: photos, voices, video. Face unlock. Voice assistants.
7. "Say the family": "Generative AI is built on deep learning. Deep learning is a kind of machine learning. Machine learning is a kind of AI."

**Script:**

[ON SCREEN: Slide 1, title]

So far, we have met two words: AI and machine learning. You may already be wondering how they fit together. And in the news you will also hear deep learning, and generative AI. It can feel like a lot of names for the same thing.

They are not the same thing. But they are related, and the relationship is simple. In this video, I will show you how they fit, using one picture: circles inside circles.

[PAUSE]

[ON SCREEN: Slide 2, taxi, car, vehicle]

Let us start with something that has nothing to do with computers.

Think of a taxi. Every taxi is a car. Now think of a car. Every car is a vehicle. But is every vehicle a car? No. A boda boda is a vehicle, and it is not a car. A bus is a vehicle, and it is not a car.

So we can draw it as circles. A small circle for taxis, inside a bigger circle for cars, inside an even bigger circle for vehicles.

Every taxi is a car. Every car is a vehicle. But not every vehicle is a car.

Keep that picture in your head, because the AI family works in exactly the same way.

[PAUSE]

[ON SCREEN: Slide 3, the AI family]

Here is the biggest circle: AI. A computer doing a job that normally needs human thinking.

Inside it, a smaller circle: machine learning. This is the kind of AI that learns from examples instead of written rules. Machine learning is a kind of AI. But not all AI is machine learning. The old rule-based spam filter, with its hand-written rules, is AI too, and it is not machine learning.

Inside machine learning, a smaller circle again: deep learning. Deep learning is a kind of machine learning.

And inside deep learning, the smallest circle for now: generative AI. Generative AI is built on deep learning.

Let me say the whole chain in one go. Generative AI is built on deep learning. Deep learning is a kind of machine learning. Machine learning is a kind of AI.

[PAUSE]

If you remember the taxi, you can always rebuild this. Generative AI is like the taxi. Deep learning is like the car. Machine learning is like the vehicle. And AI is like everything that moves on the road.

Now, let us look inside the circle called deep learning, because it is where the most exciting results come from.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 4, layers that build up]

Deep learning uses layers. Let me explain with a picture of a face.

Imagine your phone is trying to recognise a face. The photo comes in. It is just a huge grid of tiny dots of colour.

The first layer looks at the dots, and finds edges and lines. Here is a line, there is a curve. Nothing more than that.

The second layer takes those edges and lines, and finds shapes. A circle. An oval. A curve above a curve.

The third layer takes the shapes and says, that looks like an eye.

And the fourth layer puts the eyes, the nose and the mouth together and says, that is a face.

[PAUSE]

Each layer builds on the one before it. Simple things at the start, more meaningful things at the end. That is why it is called deep. The more layers, the deeper.

Think of how a tailor looks at a customer. First she sees the shape of a shoulder, then the length of an arm, then she sees the whole size of the person. She builds up from small details to a full picture. A little like that.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 5, what it is]

So what is deep learning, exactly? It is a kind of computer system called a neural network. A neural network is loosely inspired by how brain cells connect to each other.

But I want to be clear about this. It is only loosely inspired. It is maths, not a brain. There are no real brain cells in your phone. The word neural can mislead you, so please remember: it is maths, not a brain.

[PAUSE]

[ON SCREEN: Slide 6, what it is good at]

What is deep learning good at? It is good at messy data.

What do I mean by messy? Photos. Voices. Video. This kind of data does not come in neat rows and columns like a table of prices. A photo is millions of dots. A voice is a wave of sound. It is hard to write rules for these. It is very hard to say, in words, exactly what makes a face a face.

But deep learning, with its layers, can find the patterns by itself. That is why face unlock uses it. That is why voice assistants use it.

So when you speak to your phone, and it understands what you said, even with a noisy street behind you, deep learning is likely to be at work.

[PAUSE]

[ON SCREEN: Slide 7, say the family]

Let us practise the family once more, using examples.

Your spam filter learning from marked messages: that is machine learning.

Face unlock, finding edges, then shapes, then a face: that is deep learning, which is a kind of machine learning.

A chatbot writing a message for you: that is generative AI, which is built on deep learning.

And all of these are AI.

[PAUSE]

Here is a question to think about. A shop owner says, "I use AI to sort my customers' photos." Is she using machine learning? She could be. Is she using deep learning? She probably is, because photos are messy data. Is she using generative AI? Not if it only sorts. Generative AI creates, and that is the topic of the next video.

You do not need to memorise every detail. Just keep the circles in mind. AI contains machine learning, which contains deep learning, which contains generative AI.

**Pause-and-try prompt:** Draw the four circles on a piece of paper, from AI on the outside to generative AI on the inside. Then write one example in each circle, such as a hand-written rule filter, a spam filter that learns from examples, face unlock and a chatbot.

**Recap line:** AI contains machine learning, which contains deep learning, which contains generative AI, and deep learning is strong with messy data such as photos, voices and video.

We have reached the innermost circle: generative AI. This is the kind of AI that people are talking about most, and the kind you will use most in this course. So let us take our time.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 2, recognises and creates]

First, let us look at what AI did before generative AI. What we now call traditional AI recognises things. It sorts, scores and predicts.

Is this message spam, or not spam? Is this a face, or not a face? Is this payment fraud, or not fraud? In each case, the AI looks at something that already exists, and gives you a label, a number, or a choice from a list.

Generative AI does something different. It creates.

It writes text. It makes pictures. It writes computer code. It composes music. It produces something that did not exist a moment ago.

Computers used to be good at sorting. Now they can also draft and create. That is the new thing we mentioned earlier. Remember, AI is old, but this one kind is new.

[PAUSE]

[ON SCREEN: Slide 3, it generates]

Now, here is a point that surprises people. When you ask a generative AI a question, it does not look up the answer and paste it. It is not like a search through a huge library, where it finds a page and copies it out.

Instead, it generates the answer. It builds it fresh, from patterns it learned.

Imagine a bakery owner in Entebbe who asks a chatbot for a short message to customers about a Saturday cake order. The chatbot does not have a ready message stored in a drawer. It writes one, word by word, using patterns it learned about how such messages usually go.

And that is also why, if she asks the exact same thing again, she will get different words.

[PAUSE]

[ON SCREEN: Slide 4, large language model]

Now let me explain the name that you will hear again and again: large language model. People shorten it to LLM.

Let us take it word by word.

Large. It learned from a massive amount of text.

Language. It works with words.

Model. This is just the name for the system.

So a large language model is a system that learned from a massive amount of text, and works with words. It is the engine behind ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude.

When we say chatbot, we mean the part you talk to. A large language model is the engine inside it. In this course, I will usually say chatbot, and I will say LLM when I want to talk about the engine.

See what it wrote? A sentence I did not type. It guessed the next word, then the next, then the next. Sometimes it makes sense, and sometimes it is a little silly.

Your phone is guessing the next word. A language model does the same thing, on a much bigger scale. It predicts the next word, then the next, then the next.

Now, a note on the scale. Your phone's suggestion looks at a few words. A large language model has learned from a massive amount of text, so its guesses are far better. That is why it can write a whole polite message, and not just a silly sentence.

[PAUSE]

[ON SCREEN: Slide 6, sounding right]

This brings me to the most important sentence in this whole module.

Sounding right is not the same as being right.

Think about what the engine is doing. It is predicting what fits. It is not checking what is true. So, it can be wrong, and completely sure at the same time.

Here is why this matters for you. Say a student asks a chatbot for a fact for an assignment. The answer comes back in clear, confident words. It sounds like a textbook. But the chatbot was only predicting words that fit. It did not look in a textbook. The answer could be wrong, and nothing in the tone would warn you.

So, you stay in charge. And you check. We will spend a whole module on how to check, later in the course. For now, hold on to the sentence: sounding right is not the same as being right.
