## m5-l1 | How bias gets into AI | target 7 min

**Learning objective:** Explain, in plain words, why AI can inherit unfairness from the human data it learned from, using the Amazon hiring tool as one real example.

**Slides to prepare:**
1. Title: "How bias gets into AI". One line: "Limit 2: Bias".
2. "AI learns from human data, so it can inherit human bias." Three boxes: It learns from what people wrote / Human history is unequal / So answers can be unfair.
3. "It can also know less about some people, places and languages."
4. "A real example: the hiring tool." Amazon, ten years of CVs, mostly from men, scrapped. "Reported by Reuters, 2018."
5. "Nobody told it to be unfair. It copied a pattern in the data."

**Script:**

Welcome back.

In Module 4 we saw that AI can sound completely sure and still be wrong. In this lesson we look at a second limit. It is called bias.

[ON SCREEN: slide 1]

Let me explain the word first. Bias means an unfair pattern in an AI's answers. A pattern that treats some people better than others, or leaves some people out.

I want to say something before we go on. This topic can feel heavy. It does not need to. Bias is something you can notice, and something you can reduce. That is what the next two lessons are for.

Let us begin with where AI gets its knowledge.

[ON SCREEN: slide 2]

AI learns from human data. A chatbot such as ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude learned from what people wrote. Books, websites, posts and records. Millions and millions of words.

Now here is the important part. Human history is unequal. For a long time, some jobs were done mostly by men. Some groups had more money, more schooling and more voice than others. Some people wrote a great deal, and some were written about, but rarely asked.

All of that is sitting in the data. Old patterns and old stereotypes come along with it.

So the answers can be unfair. They can be sexist. They can be racist. Or they can simply leave some people out.

Notice what I am not saying. I am not saying the AI has opinions. It has none. It is copying patterns it was shown.

Think of a young tailor in Entebbe who learns by watching one senior tailor for ten years. Suppose that senior tailor only ever sewed for one kind of customer. The apprentice will become very good at sewing for that kind of customer. And a little clumsy with everyone else.

Nobody told the apprentice to be unfair. That is just what they saw. AI is a bit like that apprentice. It becomes skilled at what it saw most.

[PAUSE]

There is a second part to this.

[ON SCREEN: slide 3]

AI can also know less about some people, places and languages. If very little has been written about a village, a dialect, or a language such as Luganda, then there is less for the AI to learn from. Its answers may be thinner, or less accurate.

So here is a habit I would like you to build. Test it on what you know. Ask it about your own town. Your own school. Your own language. You are the expert on those, so you will see quickly how well it does. And if the text is important, ask a fluent speaker to check it.

Now let me show you a small example live. I will ask a chatbot something very simple.

[DEMO: Open a chatbot. Type exactly: "Write two sentences about a doctor and two sentences about a nurse." Read the answer aloud. Point out any assumption about who is a man or a woman. If it made none, say so calmly.]

Results vary. Sometimes a chatbot makes an assumption, and sometimes it does not. I cannot promise what you will see on your own phone today. That is fine. The skill is not catching the AI out. The skill is looking.

[PAUSE]

Now, a real example.

[ON SCREEN: slide 4]

Amazon built an experimental tool to rate job applicants. It learned from ten years of past CVs. A CV, as you know, is the document where you list your education and work. Most of those CVs came from men.

The tool began marking down CVs that mentioned women. Amazon scrapped it. This was reported by Reuters in 2018.

[ON SCREEN: slide 5]

Look at the line on the slide. Nobody told it to be unfair. It copied a pattern in the data.

The past data was mostly men. So the pattern the tool found favoured men. It did exactly what its training taught it, and that was the problem.

Let me bring that closer to home. Please keep in mind that this next example is only an illustration, not a real case. Imagine a company in Kampala receiving five hundred CVs for one job. A busy manager is tempted to let a tool sort them. If that tool learned from the company's past hires, and those hires mostly came from one background, it may keep choosing the same kind of person. Not because anyone wanted that. Because that is the pattern it saw.

And think of the person on the other side. A young woman from a small town, with a strong CV, who never gets called. She does not know why. Nobody does. That is why we take this seriously, and why we keep a human involved when decisions are about people.

[PAUSE]

Let me pull the three ideas together.

First, AI learns from what people wrote.

Second, human history is unequal, so the data is unequal too.

Third, nobody has to tell AI to be unfair. It copies the patterns in its data.

And one more small thing. AI can know less about some people, places and languages. So test it on what you know.

One more point on the hiring story. It is easy to say, the tool was foolish. But look at it differently. The tool was a mirror. It showed the company a pattern that was already in its own history. When we notice a pattern like that, we have a chance to change it. That is a hopeful way to see it.

None of this means you should be afraid of AI. It means you read its answers the way a good editor reads a draft. With attention. Asking, who is in this picture, and who is missing?

In the next lesson I will show you exactly how to do that.

**Pause-and-try prompt:** Pick something you know very well, such as your own town, school or language, and ask a chatbot a few questions about it. Note one thing it got right and one thing that felt thin, wrong or left out.

**Recap line:** AI learns from what people wrote, so it can copy old unfair patterns, and nobody has to tell it to.

**Bridge:** Next, we will practise spotting bias in a real answer and learn four habits that reduce it.

## m5-l2 | Spot bias and reduce it | target 6 min

**Learning objective:** Read an AI answer for hidden assumptions and use four habits to reduce bias.

**Slides to prepare:**
1. Title: "Spot bias and reduce it".
2. "Try it now: Spot the assumption". The prompt: "Write a short story about a nurse, a boda boda rider and a company director."
3. Four questions to ask while reading: Who is a man or a woman? What names? Which region or tribe? Who is rich?
4. "Four habits": Look for who is missing / Never let AI decide alone about people / Say what you want / Ask people who would be affected.
5. "Results vary. The skill is looking."

**Script:**

Welcome back.

In the last lesson we saw how bias gets into AI. Now we practise spotting it, and then we learn four habits that reduce it. You can do all of this with an ordinary chatbot on your phone.

[ON SCREEN: slide 1]

Let us start with a short exercise. It takes less than two minutes.

[ON SCREEN: slide 2]

I am going to ask a chatbot for a very short story. Three characters. A nurse, a boda boda rider and a company director.

[DEMO: Open a chatbot. Type exactly: "Write a short story about a nurse, a boda boda rider and a company director."]

While it writes, remember what we are doing. We are not trying to prove that AI is bad. We are reading carefully, the way you would read a draft from a new colleague.

[PAUSE]

Let me say why this exercise is worth doing. A story is a small, safe place to see how an AI fills in blanks. I gave it only three jobs. I did not say who these people are. So whatever it adds, the names, the genders, the home towns, the money, it added by itself. Those additions show me the patterns it leans on.

Now I read the story, slowly. And I ask four questions.

[ON SCREEN: slide 3]

Who is a man, and who is a woman? Is the nurse a woman? Is the boda boda rider a man? Is the director a man?

What names did it choose? Do all the names come from one region?

Which region or tribe do the characters seem to come from? Does the story say, or does it only suggest?

And who is rich? Who is poor? Who has the power in this story, and who is helping?

[DEMO: Read the generated story aloud. Point out any assumptions you see, and say plainly if you see none. Do not exaggerate.]

Here is something important. Results vary. Some days a chatbot makes these assumptions, and some days it does not. If I run this again tomorrow, I may see something different. So do not worry if your story looks fair. The skill is not finding bias every time. The skill is looking, every time.

Now the second step. I tell the chatbot what I want.

[DEMO: Type exactly: "Rewrite this without assuming anyone's gender, tribe or background."]

[PAUSE]

Let us read the new version. Is it better? Compare it with the first one. Did the names change? Did the roles change? Did the story still work?

Is it better? Sometimes yes. Sometimes it only changes a few words. Then you can say more. You are in charge of the conversation.

Now let me give you the four habits. They are simple.

[ON SCREEN: slide 4]

Habit one. Look for who is missing, or who is shown as a stereotype. If every doctor in the answer is a man and every cleaner is a woman, that is worth noticing. If a story about a market has no one from outside the capital, ask why.

Habit two. Never let AI decide alone about people. This matters most for hiring, marks, loans and discipline. These decisions change lives. Imagine a headteacher who lets a tool decide which students get a place, with no teacher reviewing it. Or a small lender who lets a tool decide who gets a loan. The tool might be useful as one input. But a person must look at the result, ask questions and take responsibility.

Habit three. Say what you want. You can write it in your prompt. For example: "Use varied names and roles." Or: "Include women and men in different jobs." The chatbot cannot read your mind, but it can follow your instruction.

Habit four. Ask the people who would be affected. They notice what you miss.

Let me give a Ugandan example. Say you run a bakery in Entebbe and you use AI to write a job advert for a sales assistant. Before you post it, show it to two or three people. A young woman. A young man. Someone who speaks Luganda at home. Ask them one question: does this advert feel like it is for me? Their answers will teach you more than any tool.

[PAUSE]

Let me say the four habits once more, slowly.

Look for who is missing. Never let AI decide alone about people. Say what you want. Ask the people who would be affected.

Two small cautions. First, do not swing too far the other way and force every story into a pattern. The aim is a fair and natural picture, not a script. Second, remember that you are the editor. The chatbot offers a draft. You decide what goes out under your name.

You do not need to be perfect. You only need to look, and to keep a person in charge.

**Pause-and-try prompt:** Open a chatbot and type: "Write a short story about a nurse, a boda boda rider and a company director." Read it for assumptions about gender, names, region or tribe, and who is rich. Then ask: "Rewrite this without assuming anyone's gender, tribe or background," and compare.

**Recap line:** Read every answer for who is missing, keep a person in charge of decisions about people, say what you want, and ask the people who will be affected.

**Bridge:** Next, we turn from fairness to privacy, and ask what happens to the words you type.

## m5-l3 | Your words may not stay private | target 7 min

**Learning objective:** Explain why public AI tools are not a safe place for private information, and recognise personal information (PII) when you see it.

**Slides to prepare:**
1. Title: "Your words may not stay private". "Limit 3: Privacy".
2. "Public AI tools may use what you type to train their models, and could reveal it later."
3. "Never put in: confidential work information, passwords, personal information. No exceptions."
4. "PII: anything that identifies a person." List: a name with a phone number, an ID number, an address, a photo, medical details, bank or mobile money details.
5. A made-up message with PII highlighted (see the demo below).
6. "Is deleting my chat enough? Not always."

**Script:**

Welcome back.

So far we have looked at what AI gets wrong, and how bias gets in. Now we turn to something that depends on you. Privacy.

[ON SCREEN: slide 1]

Here is the main message of this lesson, in one sentence. Your words may not stay private.

[ON SCREEN: slide 2]

Let me explain. A public AI tool is an AI service that anyone can use. A free chatbot is a good example. Public tools may use what you type to train their models, and they could reveal it later.

I want to be careful here. I am not saying that every tool does this. I am not saying it will happen to you. Policies differ from one tool to another. I am saying it is possible, and that you cannot always be sure. So the safe habit is to decide, before you type, what is safe to share.

Think of typing into a public chatbot like speaking in a busy market. You can chat about prices, about the weather, about the football. But you would not read out your mobile money PIN to the whole market, even if you believed nobody was listening.

[PAUSE]

Why does this matter so much for us? Many of us use free tools, on shared phones, on public Wi-Fi, in internet cafes, because data is expensive. A free tool is useful, and I use them. But free does not mean private. It simply means you did not pay in money. So we pay attention instead.

[ON SCREEN: slide 3]

So here is the rule. Never put in confidential work information. Never put in passwords. Never put in personal information. No exceptions.

Let me explain each one.

Confidential work information is anything your employer or your business would not want outside. Unreleased results. A price list for a tender. A contract. Staff records.

Passwords and PINs. Your email password. Your mobile money PIN. Your bank login. These never go into a chatbot. Ever.

And personal information. This has a special name, so let me introduce it.

[ON SCREEN: slide 4]

It is called PII. P, I, I. It stands for personally identifiable information. Personal information, in plain words. PII is anything that identifies a person.

Here are examples. A name together with a phone number. An ID number. An address. A photo. Medical details. Bank or mobile money details.

Notice something. A name on its own may not say very much. Many people are called Sarah. But a name together with a phone number points to one person. That is why the combination matters.

[PAUSE]

Let me show you a message. I made it up for this video. It is not a real person.

[ON SCREEN: slide 5]

[DEMO: Show this made-up message on the slide: "Please write a reply to Sarah Namukasa, 0772 000 000, ID number CM00000000000. She says her mobile money payment of UGX 150,000 did not reach her account." Highlight the name, phone number, ID number and the payment detail.]

Look closely. How many pieces of personal information can you find? Pause the video and count them.

[PAUSE]

I count four. The name. The phone number. The ID number. And the payment detail. Any one of these could be sensitive. Together, they identify one real person and their money.

If this were a real message from a customer, pasting it into a public chatbot would be a mistake. The customer trusted you with those details. They did not agree to share them with a tool.

Notice also what is not on the list. Your favourite food is not. The name of a famous footballer is not. A made-up character is not. Most of what you type will be perfectly fine. The list is short, and it is specific.

Let me give you two more everyday situations.

A tailor in Entebbe keeps a notebook of customers. Names, phone numbers, measurements. It would be tempting to photograph the pages and ask a chatbot to organise them. But that notebook is full of personal information. The tailor can still ask for help, and I will show you how in the next lesson.

Or a student who is writing about a family illness. A medical report has details that belong to one person. Those details should stay out of a public tool.

Now, a question I hear often.

[ON SCREEN: slide 6]

"Is deleting my chat enough?" The honest answer is, not always. Policies differ by tool. Deleting may help, but you cannot be sure what happened before you deleted it. The safe move is not to paste secrets in the first place.

And another one. "I changed a setting, so now it is private." Be careful. Many tools let you limit how your chats are used, and it is good to check. In Module 2 we looked for that settings page. But a setting is not permission to paste secrets. It reduces risk. It does not remove it.

[PAUSE]

Let me bring this together.

Public AI tools may use what you type, and could reveal it later.

Never paste passwords, PINs, ID numbers or confidential work information.

PII is anything that identifies a person. A name with a phone number, an ID number, an address, a photo, medical details, bank or mobile money details.

Here is a thought to take with you. When you type something into a chatbot, you are not only protecting yourself. You are protecting everyone whose details you hold. Your customers. Your classmates. Your patients. Your family.

That is a real responsibility, and it is a small one to carry. It only needs a pause before you press send.

In the next lesson, I will show you how to get the help you need, without sharing what you should not.

**Pause-and-try prompt:** Look back at the last few things you typed into any chatbot. Did any of them contain a password, an ID number, or a name with a phone number? If so, make a note to describe the situation without those details next time.

**Recap line:** Public AI tools may keep and use what you type, so personal and confidential information stays out.

**Bridge:** Next, we will learn safer habits and three simple rules, so you can still get help without sharing secrets.

## m5-l4 | Safer habits and three rules | target 6 min

**Learning objective:** Get help from AI without sharing personal or confidential information, using "instead of this, do this" habits and three rules.

**Slides to prepare:**
1. Title: "Safer habits and three rules".
2. "Instead of this, do this" table, three rows (see script).
3. "Three rules": Use approved, secure tools / Check the settings / Not sure? Do not paste it.
4. "Asking costs a minute. A leak can cost far more."

**Script:**

Welcome back.

In the last lesson we learned that your words may not stay private. Now the useful question. How do you still get help from AI, without sharing what you should not?

The answer is simple. Describe the problem, not the person. Let me show you with three examples.

[ON SCREEN: slide 2]

Example one. A customer sends a complaint. It has their name and their phone number. The temptation is to paste the whole message into a chatbot and ask for a reply.

Instead of that, describe it. Let me do this live.

[DEMO: Open a chatbot. Type exactly: "A customer says their delivery was two days late. Write a polite reply."]

Look at the answer. It is polite, and it is useful. And the chatbot knows nothing about who the customer is. I can add the name myself, by hand, in my own message. The chatbot never saw it.

[PAUSE]

Did you notice how little I lost? I still got a clear, polite reply. The only thing missing was personal information, and I did not need it.

Example two. Imagine you run a small shop. You have a customer list with names and phone numbers, and you want to find patterns. Who buys most often? Who stopped coming?

Instead of pasting the list, ask how to do the analysis. And practise on made-up data. Let me show you.

[DEMO: Type exactly: "I have a customer list with columns for name, items bought and date. How do I find which customers buy most often? Show me using five made-up customers."]

Now I have the method, and I tried it on made-up customers. When I am happy that I understand it, I can do the real work in my own spreadsheet, on my own phone or laptop. Nobody's real details travelled anywhere.

[PAUSE]

Example three. A full contract, with names and amounts. Perhaps a supplier agreement for UGX 8,000,000 with a named company.

Instead of pasting all of it, remove the names and the amounts first. Replace them with words like "Supplier" and "Amount". Or, if your organisation has an approved tool, use that. Which brings me to the three rules.

[ON SCREEN: slide 3]

Rule one. Use approved, secure tools. Your organisation may provide one. If you work for a company, a school or a clinic, ask whether there is a tool they have approved. For anything sensitive, use that.

Rule two. Check the settings. Many tools let you limit how your chats are used. It is worth finding that page and reading it. But remember what we said in the last lesson. A setting is not permission to paste secrets.

Rule three. Not sure? Do not paste it.

[ON SCREEN: slide 4]

And here is the line I want you to remember. Asking costs a minute. A leak can cost far more.

Let me make that real. Say you are an assistant in a small office, and you are not sure whether a document is safe to put into a chatbot. You could ask your supervisor. That takes a minute. Perhaps she says yes. Perhaps she says no. Either way, you have lost almost nothing. But if you paste first and ask later, and the document was confidential, you cannot take it back. A minute of asking is cheap.

[PAUSE]

A word about speed. When you are busy, it feels faster to paste everything. It is not. Describing the problem takes about ten seconds longer. After a week it becomes a habit, and you will not even notice.

Let me also say what you can still do. A lot. You can ask for a reply to a complaint. You can ask for a polite message, a sample budget, a lesson plan, an outline of a talk. You can ask how to do something with a spreadsheet. All of that works without any personal or confidential details. Privacy does not mean you cannot use AI. It means you give it the problem, and keep the people out of it.

And what about your own information? The same care applies. Your mobile money PIN, your passwords and your ID number stay out, even when you are only asking for help with your own affairs. If a chatbot ever asks you for a PIN or a password, stop. That is a sign something is wrong.

Before we finish, a small test. A customer has sent you a message with their name, phone number and a complaint about a missing order. What do you type into the chatbot?

[PAUSE]

You type something like: "A customer says their order did not arrive. Write a polite reply and ask them to confirm their delivery address." No name. No number. Just the problem.

Let me say the three rules one more time. Use approved, secure tools. Check the settings. Not sure? Do not paste it.

**Pause-and-try prompt:** Think of one real message, note or document you might paste into a chatbot. Rewrite it so it describes the problem without any names, phone numbers, ID numbers or amounts, then send that version.

**Recap line:** Describe the problem, not the person, and when you are not sure, do not paste it.

**Bridge:** That finishes Module 5. In Module 6 we will put everything together into one simple guide: green, amber, red.

## m6-l1 | Green, amber, red | target 8 min

**Learning objective:** Sort everyday uses of AI into green (go), amber (check carefully) and red (stop), and ask four questions of any AI tool.

**Slides to prepare:**
1. Title: "Green, amber, red".
2. Three columns. GREEN, low risk, go: brainstorm names and ideas / draft a thank-you note / explain a concept simply / summarise a public article. AMBER, check carefully: facts, numbers and quotes / health, legal and money information / anything that goes out under your name. RED, stop: pasting personal or confidential information into a public tool / letting AI alone decide about people / submitting AI work you cannot explain, or that your school or employer does not allow.
3. "Your turn: green, amber or red?" Six cases, numbered 1 to 6.
4. "The answers": one line each.
5. "Four questions to ask of any AI tool": Does it do good for people? Is it built and tested for safety? Is it fair? Is someone accountable?
6. Small note on the slide: "From Google's original 2018 AI principles. Restated in February 2025 as three pillars."

**Script:**

Welcome to Module 6.

Over the last five modules you have learned a lot. What AI is. How to ask it clearly. How to put it to work. How to check it. How to notice bias and protect privacy.

Today I want to give you one simple tool that pulls all of that together. It works like a traffic light. Green, amber, red.

[ON SCREEN: slide 1]

When you are about to use AI for something, you ask yourself, which colour is this?

[ON SCREEN: slide 2]

Green means low risk. Go.

Here are green examples. Brainstorming names and ideas. Drafting a thank-you note. Explaining a concept in simple words. Summarising a public article.

Why are these green? Because if the AI makes a small mistake, little harm is done. You can see the result, judge it and improve it. A baker in Entebbe who asks for ten name ideas for a new cake is on green. If nine are poor, nothing is lost.

Green does not mean you stop thinking. It means the risk is low.

Amber means check carefully.

Amber examples. Facts, numbers and quotes. Health, legal and money information. And anything that goes out under your name.

Remember hallucinations from Module 4. If an answer contains a number, a date or a quote, treat it as a claim to check. And if you will send something to a customer, a lecturer or an employer, it carries your name. You are responsible for it, so you read it carefully first.

Red means stop.

Red examples. Pasting personal or confidential information into a public tool. Letting AI alone decide about people. And submitting AI work you cannot explain, or that your school or employer does not allow.

That last one matters for students. If your university or school has a rule about AI, follow it. And even where AI is allowed, if you hand in something you cannot explain in your own words, it is not really your work, and it will not help you learn.

[PAUSE]

One thing about the colours. They are not fixed for ever. A task can move. Summarising a public article is green. But if you then use that summary to quote figures in a report with your name on it, those figures move to amber, because now you must check them. Asking the colour question again, at each step, is the habit.

Now let us practise. I will show you six cases. For each, I want you to decide, green, amber or red, and say why. Pause the video after each one if you need to.

[ON SCREEN: slide 3]

Case one. Asking AI for name ideas for your new shop.

[PAUSE]

Case two. Pasting a customer's ID details to fill a form faster.

[PAUSE]

Case three. Asking AI what documents you need to register a business.

[PAUSE]

Case four. Asking AI for a cover letter draft, then editing it.

[PAUSE]

Case five. Following an AI's medicine dose for a child without checking.

[PAUSE]

Case six. Pasting your company's unreleased results to get a summary.

[PAUSE]

Let us go through the answers.

[ON SCREEN: slide 4]

One is green. Name ideas for a shop are low risk. Pick the ones you like.

Two is red. Personal information never goes into a public tool. A customer's ID details belong to them, and you were trusted with them.

Three is amber. It is a useful start. AI can give you a list to begin with. But rules change, and AI can be wrong, so you check the official source.

Four is green, with a note. A cover letter draft is a good use. Edit it, make it sound like you, and check that every fact about you is true.

Five is red. Health matters. Never follow a medicine dose from a chatbot without checking. For anything about medicine, ask a doctor or pharmacist.

Six is red. Unreleased company results are confidential. Use only an approved, secure tool, if at all.

Look at what the six answers have in common. Green is where little can go wrong. Amber is where the AI is useful, but the final responsibility is yours. Red is where the harm to a person, or to your job, can be serious or hard to undo.

How did you do? If you disagreed with one, that is fine. The point is not to memorise a list. The point is to ask the question before you act.

[PAUSE]

There is one more thing to add. The colours work for tasks. But sometimes you are choosing a tool, or you are asked whether your school or your company should use one. For that, here are four questions to ask of any AI tool.

[ON SCREEN: slide 5]

Question one. Does it do good for people?

Question two. Is it built and tested for safety? Or was it rushed out untested?

Question three. Is it fair? Does it avoid creating or reinforcing unfair bias?

Question four. Is someone accountable? Does a real person or company answer to the people it affects?

[ON SCREEN: slide 6]

For your information, the teaching deck notes that these four ideas come from Google's original 2018 AI principles. In February 2025, Google restated its principles as three pillars: bold innovation, responsible development and deployment, and collaborative progress. You do not need to remember that. What matters is that the four questions are practical, and you can ask them of any tool.

Think of a mobile money agent. You trust an agent because there is a shop, a name, and a place to complain if something goes wrong. You can see who is accountable. Ask the same of an AI tool. If it makes a serious mistake, who answers to the person who was harmed?

These questions are useful for a school or a small business too. A headteacher choosing a tool for marking, or a shop owner choosing a tool for customer messages, can ask all four before saying yes.

[PAUSE]

Let me close with the guide again.

Green. Low risk. Go.

Amber. Check carefully.

Red. Stop.

And four questions for any tool. Does it do good? Is it safe? Is it fair? Is someone accountable?

**Pause-and-try prompt:** Write down three things you have done or want to do with AI this month, and label each one green, amber or red. For every amber or red one, note what you will check or change.

**Recap line:** Before you use AI, ask which colour it is: green go, amber check, red stop.

## m6-l2 | Stay the human in the loop, and your 30-day plan | target 7 min

**Learning objective:** Describe what it means to stay the human in the loop, and leave with a simple 30-day plan for using AI well.

**Slides to prepare:**
1. Title: "Stay the human in the loop".
2. "AI is powerful. It has no human judgment, empathy or common sense. A person reviews and decides before anything affects real people."
3. "You verify the facts. You protect privacy. You make the decision."
