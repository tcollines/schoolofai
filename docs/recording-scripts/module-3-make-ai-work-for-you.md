## m3-l1 | AI is your co-pilot, not your replacement | target 6 min

**Learning objective:** The learner can explain that AI is a co-pilot for four everyday jobs, and that "AI drafts, you decide" is the rule for all of them.

**Slides to prepare:**
1. Title: "AI is your co-pilot, not your replacement".
2. Four short lines: Saves time on first drafts. A partner when you are stuck. Organises complex information fast. Handles simple tasks, no coding.
3. Large text: "AI drafts. You decide."
4. Three checks: Is it true? Is it right for my reader? Is it safe to share?
5. What never goes into a chatbot (five short lines, taken from the deck).

**Script:**

Welcome back. In Module 2 you learned to ask a chatbot clearly, using context, task and format. Now we put that skill to work.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 1, title]

Picture a boda boda rider on the road through Entebbe. The rider holds the handlebars. The rider chooses the route, watches the traffic, and decides when to stop.

Now imagine the rider has a map on the phone, suggesting a quicker way round a jam. The map helps. But the rider is still the one riding.

That is how I want you to think about AI. We have a word for it: co-pilot. A co-pilot is AI that helps while you stay in charge. It is not a replacement for you. It is a helper beside you.

[PAUSE]

[ON SCREEN: Slide 2, the four lines appear one at a time]

So what can a co-pilot do for you? In this module we look at four things.

First, it saves time on first drafts. The hardest part of a message or a report is often the first line. A chatbot can give you a first version in seconds. A draft means a first version, not the final one.

Second, it is a partner when you are stuck. You do not know what to call your new shop. You do not know how to start a talk. You can ask for ideas, and keep asking.

Third, it organises complex information fast. A long document or a page of messy notes can be turned into short, clear points.

Fourth, it handles simple tasks without code. For example, you can ask for a spreadsheet formula in plain words. You do not need to be a programmer.

Those four become our four jobs: brainstorm, draft, summarise and automate. Each one gets its own video, in that order.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 3, "AI drafts. You decide."]

Now the most important idea in the whole module. Four words. AI drafts. You decide.

Let me show you what that looks like.

[DEMO: Open a chatbot such as ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude. Type exactly: "I run a small bakery in Entebbe. Write a short, friendly email to a customer confirming their cake order for Saturday, UGX 120,000, pickup at 10am. Keep it under 100 words." A good result is a short, friendly email, under 100 words, that mentions Saturday, UGX 120,000 and 10am. Keep the real order details on screen beside it, for example in a notes app.]

This is the bakery request from Module 2. Context, task and format. And here is the answer, in a few seconds.

[PAUSE]

It looks good. But I do not send it yet. I read it slowly. I compare it with my real order. Does it say Saturday? Does it say UGX 120,000? Does it say ten in the morning?

[DEMO: Point to each detail on the email and tick it against the order notes. If the chatbot has changed or added something, for example a different time or a promise of delivery, point it out and delete it. If everything is correct, say so, and still add the baker's own name at the end.]

Chatbots can slip. Sometimes a detail is changed. Sometimes a sentence is added that I never said, such as a promise of delivery that my bakery does not offer. Remember what you learned in Module 1. A chatbot predicts the next word. Sounding right is not the same as being right.

So the work is shared. The AI does the quick part, the first version. I do the part that needs a person: I read it, I fix it, and I decide whether it goes out.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 4, the three checks]

Before I use anything from a chatbot, I ask three questions.

Is it true? I check the facts, the names and the numbers.

Is it right for my reader? Does the tone suit them? Does it sound like me?

And is it safe to share? That one is about what goes in, and what comes out.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 5, what never goes into a chatbot]

On that third question, here is a rule I want you to remember from day one. Some things never go into a public chatbot. A public chatbot is one that anyone can use, such as the free versions of ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude. These tools may use what you type.

So never type your passwords. Never type a PIN, and that includes your mobile money PIN. Never type national ID or passport numbers. Never type bank or card details. Never type other people's private details, or confidential information from your work or business.

Notice that the bakery request had none of that. It had a day, an amount and a time. No customer name, no phone number. When you need help with something that involves a person, describe the problem, not the person.

We cover this properly in Module 5. For now, keep the rule simple. If you are not sure, do not paste it.

[PAUSE]

Here is how the next four videos fit together. Next, we beat the blank page with brainstorming. Then we draft. Then we summarise long and messy things. Then we automate small jobs without writing code.

In every one of them, you will do three things. You will ask. You will check. You will make the result your own.

I also want you to know that using AI is a skill you build, like riding a boda boda. Nobody is born able to do it. You get better with each task you try.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 3 again]

AI is not here to take the handlebars. It is here to help you get there faster.

**Pause-and-try prompt:** Open your chatbot and write down one real task from your week that fits each of the four jobs: brainstorm, draft, summarise and automate. Keep the list. You will use it in the next four videos. (The content file has no "try it now" for this lesson, so this is a short warm-up task.)

**Recap line:** AI is your co-pilot: it drafts, and you read, check and decide.

**Bridge:** Let us start with the job that helps most when you are stuck: beating the blank page.

## m3-l2 | Job 1: Beat the blank page (brainstorm) | target 8 min

**Learning objective:** The learner can use a chatbot to produce many ideas at once, pick the best two, and refine one of them into something they can actually use.

**Slides to prepare:**
1. Title: "Job 1: Beat the blank page".
2. Ask for... 10 taglines for a small business. An outline for a talk. A meeting agenda. Interview questions.
3. Example prompt: "Generate 5 meeting agenda items for a project kickoff."
4. Try it now: the school fundraiser prompt, pick two, "Make idea 3 cheaper and easier to run."
5. "You did not get one answer. You got options to react to. That is the real value."
6. Three habits: Ask for many. Pick two and refine. Make it yours.

**Script:**

Welcome back. Today is Job 1: brainstorm.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 1, title]

First, the word. To brainstorm is to come up with many ideas quickly, before you choose one. You do not judge the ideas yet. You collect them.

Everyone knows the blank page. You sit down to write, and the page stays empty. The cursor blinks. You do not know where to begin.

Maybe you are a tailor in Entebbe, opening a second shop. You need a name for the signboard and a short line underneath. Or you are a university student who has to give a ten-minute talk on Monday and you have no outline. Or you are a market stall owner who wants to promote a Saturday special, and you cannot think how to say it.

The problem is not that you have no brain. The problem is the empty page.

That is where a chatbot helps most. It can take you from nothing to a first draft worth reacting to. And it does it in seconds.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 2, four items]

Here is what you can ask for. Ten taglines for a small business. A tagline is a short line that sums up what you do. An outline for a talk. A meeting agenda. Or interview questions for someone you want to hire.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 3, example prompt]

Here is a simple example. Type: "Generate 5 meeting agenda items for a project kickoff." A project kickoff is the first meeting of a new project.

Look at what that prompt does. It asks for a number: five. It names the task: agenda items. It gives a little context: a project kickoff. It is short, and it works.

Now remember the three-part request from Module 2. If you add your own context and format, the ideas become more useful. Compare "Give me shop names" with "I am a tailor in Entebbe opening a second shop. Give me 10 name ideas, short enough for a signboard, in plain English." The second one gives the chatbot something to work with.

Here is the same idea for the university student. Type: "I am a second-year student. I must give a ten-minute talk on saving money. Give me an outline with five main points." Now the student has a skeleton. The student still chooses which points are true, which are useful, and which to drop. And the student adds a real story from campus life, because that is what makes a talk memorable.

Or the market stall owner. "Give me five short lines to tell customers about my Saturday special." The owner reads them, picks one, and changes a word so it sounds like the market.

Now we do it together. This is your try-it-now task, and I will do it first.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 4]

[DEMO: Open a chatbot such as ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude. Type exactly: "I am organising a school fundraiser for 400 students. Give me 10 ideas to raise money." A good result is a numbered list of ten different ideas, some cheap and some costly, some easy and some hard. For example you might see a talent show, a sponsored walk or a bake sale, but yours will differ. Do not claim any particular idea will appear.]

I typed: "I am organising a school fundraiser for 400 students. Give me 10 ideas to raise money."

And here is the answer. Ten ideas, numbered. [PAUSE]

Now I do not accept all ten. I read them. I ask myself some questions. Would this work at my school? Does it cost money before it makes money? Do we have the time and the people? Is it allowed by our school?

A chatbot does not know your school. It does not know your headteacher, your budget or your rules. So I am the one who decides.

[DEMO: Read the list aloud. Cross out two or three ideas that would not suit a school, with a reason for each. Circle the two best.]

I will pick the two I like best. Say, number 3 and number 7.

Now I do not start again. Remember Module 2: refine, do not restart. Starting over throws away what was already good. So I give a follow-up instruction.

[DEMO: Type exactly: "Make idea 3 cheaper and easier to run." A good result is a new version of idea 3 with lower costs and fewer steps, for example fewer helpers or simpler materials. Read it aloud and say whether it is realistic.]

I type: "Make idea 3 cheaper and easier to run."

And now it gives me a leaner version. Fewer things to buy. Fewer people to organise. [PAUSE]

Is it good? I check. Does it still make sense? Are there steps I know are not realistic? If a step says "get a famous musician to perform for free", I laugh and delete it.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 5]

Now notice something. You did not get one answer. You got options to react to. That is the real value.

When you react, you start to know what you want. "Not this one. This one, but cheaper. This one, but for older students." You are thinking, and the chatbot is feeding you material. That is a co-pilot at work.

You can keep going. Ask for ten more. Ask for ideas that need no money. Ask for the same idea in a different style. Each follow-up costs you a few seconds.

[PAUSE]

[ON SCREEN: Slide 6, three habits]

Let me give you three habits for this job.

One. Ask for many ideas at once. Ten is better than one, because the best idea is often number seven.

Two. Pick the best two and refine them. Do not try to polish all ten.

Three. Treat the answers as inspiration, then make them yours. Inspiration means a spark to start from. It is not the finished product. Change the words. Add what only you know about your school, your customers or your town.

Here is a warning, a small one. Because a chatbot has seen so many examples, many of its ideas can sound alike. Taglines can sound generic. If every shop on the road used the same line, nobody would remember yours. So when you find a line you like, bend it. Put your own twist on it. If you have a Luganda phrase that your customers love, use it. The chatbot can help you with Luganda, but the quality is uneven, so ask a fluent speaker to check anything important.

And a word on privacy. Brainstorming is usually safe, because you are asking for ideas, not sharing secrets. But think before you type. If you are brainstorming with real names, real phone numbers or confidential business plans, take them out first. Describe the shop, not the customer. Describe the school fundraiser, not the child. Module 5 covers this in full.

[PAUSE]

Let me say it once more. You are not asking the chatbot to decide for you. You are asking it to put ten things on the table so that you can choose.

AI drafts. You decide.

Now it is your turn.

**Pause-and-try prompt:** Pause the video and type: "I am organising a school fundraiser for 400 students. Give me 10 ideas to raise money." Pick the two ideas you like best, then ask: "Make idea 3 cheaper and easier to run." Read the new version and decide whether you would really use it.

**Recap line:** When you are stuck, ask for many ideas, pick two, refine them, and make them yours.

**Bridge:** Once you have an idea, you need to write it down, so next we look at drafting in seconds and finishing in your own voice.

**Word count:** 1006 words (about 7.5 min at 135 wpm)

## m3-l3 | Job 2: Draft in seconds, finish in your own voice | target 9 min

**Learning objective:** The learner can get a first draft from a chatbot, improve it with follow-up instructions, and edit it by hand until it is true, clear and in their own voice before sending.

**Slides to prepare:**
1. Title: "Job 2: Draft in seconds, finish in your own voice".
2. Three boxes: AI drafts (the first version, in seconds). You edit (facts, tone and your own voice). You send (only after you have read it).
3. Good for: emails, reports, social media posts. Always check: Is it clear? Does the tone suit your reader?
4. Try it now: the follow-up email prompt, "Make it warmer", "Make it shorter", then edit by hand.
5. Not quite right? Do not start again. Refine: "Make it shorter." "Make it warmer." "Explain that in simpler words." "Use a more formal tone." "Give me three versions." "Add an example."
6. "Never send the first draft. The final touches are yours."

**Script:**

Welcome back. Today is Job 2: draft.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 1, title]

A draft is a first version. It is not the final one. Think of a tailor who cuts the cloth and pins it on you before the final stitching. The pinned version is not the finished shirt. It is there so that you can see what needs to change.

A chatbot is very good at the pinned version. It can write the first version of an email, a report or a social media post in seconds. But you are the one who fits it to the customer. That is the job.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 2, three boxes]

Here is the pattern. AI drafts. You edit. You send.

AI drafts the first version, in seconds. You edit the facts, the tone and your own voice. And you send only after you have read it.

Tone means how a message feels to the reader: friendly, formal, warm, firm. The right tone for your landlord is not the right tone for your best friend.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 3]

This works well for emails, reports and social media posts. And whenever you read a draft, ask two questions. Is it clear? And does the tone suit my reader?

Now let me show you with a real situation.

Imagine I sent a quote to a client, and five days have passed. No reply. I need to follow up, but I do not want to sound pushy. Many people stay stuck here for an hour. Let us see how long it takes with a chatbot.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 4]

[DEMO: Open a chatbot such as ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude. Type exactly: "Write a polite follow-up email to a client who has not replied to my quote for five days. Keep it short and friendly." A good result is a short email with a greeting, a polite reminder about the quote, an offer to answer questions, and a closing line. Expect placeholders or invented details; point them out.]

I type: "Write a polite follow-up email to a client who has not replied to my quote for five days. Keep it short and friendly."

And here it is. [PAUSE]

Not bad. It has a greeting. It reminds the client about the quote. It offers to answer questions. It closes politely.

But I do not send it. Now the real work starts.

First, I read it. Slowly. Out loud, if I can. Is it clear? Is there anything I would never say?

Look at this sentence. It says, "As we discussed on the phone." But did we speak on the phone? No. We only sent a quote. The chatbot made that up to make the email sound natural. If I sent it, the client might think I am confused, or worse, careless.

[DEMO: Point to one invented or untrue detail in the draft, such as a phone call, a meeting, a discount or a date that is not real, and delete it. If the draft has no invented detail, point to a placeholder such as [Your Name] and fill it in.]

This is why we never send the first draft. The chatbot has never met my client. It does not know what happened. It fills gaps with what sounds likely.

Now let us improve it. Remember Module 2: do not start again, refine.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 5, list of refinements]

I type: "Make it warmer."

[DEMO: Type "Make it warmer." Read the new version aloud. A good result keeps the same facts and adds friendlier wording.]

Better. It sounds like someone who cares. Now: "Make it shorter."

[DEMO: Type "Make it shorter." A good result is a shorter email with the same key points.]

Good. A busy client will read a short message. Long ones wait for later.

You can also say: "Use a more formal tone." Or "Explain that in simpler words." Or "Give me three versions." Then you can choose the one you like. Each of these takes a few seconds.

[PAUSE]

Now the last step, and the most important. Finishing in your own voice.

Your own voice is how you really write and speak. If your client knows you, they will know when a message does not sound like you. Chatbot writing can sound stiff, or too polished, or full of phrases you would never use.

So I edit by hand. Three things.

One. Add my name. A message without my real name is just a template.

Two. Add one personal detail. Something only I would know. Perhaps, "I hope the rain did not disturb your week." Or the name of the shop, or a small detail from the last time we spoke.

Three. Fix anything untrue. Check the dates, the amounts, the names, the promises.

[DEMO: Edit the draft by hand on screen. Add a name, add one personal sentence, and remove or correct one thing that is not true. Then read the final version aloud.]

Now it reads like me. And it took minutes, not an hour.

Let me give you a few quick ways to find your own voice in a draft.

Look for stiff phrases. If a sentence sounds like a letter from a government office, and you would never say it, rewrite it in plain words.

Look for words you do not use. If the draft says "utilise" and you would say "use", change it.

Look for what is missing. A chatbot cannot know that the client likes short messages, or that you always end with "Thank you, and have a good week." You know that. Add it.

The same steps work on a social media post. Say a bakery in Entebbe wants a short post about Saturday cakes. Ask for a draft, then check the price, the day and the pickup time against the real details. Then add a line that only the baker would write.

And a student writing a report works the same way. Ask for a first structure, then write the real content in your own words, and check every fact.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 6, "Never send the first draft"]

Here is a good test. Read the message aloud. Would I say this to the client's face? If I would not, I change it.

[PAUSE]

Now, three things you should know about drafting.

First, privacy. When I asked for the email, I did not give the client's name, phone number or the amount of the quote. I wrote "a client", and I will add the details myself when I paste the draft into my own email. If you ask for a message that asks someone to send money, do not put in your mobile money PIN, your bank details or anyone's ID number. Add the payment details yourself, in your own email or message, not in the chatbot. A public chatbot may use what you type. Module 5 covers this fully.

Second, language. A chatbot can write in Luganda to a degree. But the quality is uneven. If you are writing a message in Luganda that matters, ask a fluent speaker to check it before you send it.

Third, the rules. Is using AI cheating? It depends on the rules at your school or your workplace. Follow them. And if someone asks whether you used AI, say so. If you are a student, check what your lecturer allows before you hand in anything. A draft is a tool for you. It is not a way to hand in work you do not understand.

[PAUSE]

This works for other documents too. A short report. A caption for a social media post about your bakery's Saturday cakes. A thank-you note to a customer. A message to your landlord. In every case, the steps are the same. Ask clearly. Get a draft. Read it. Refine it. Edit it by hand. Then send.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 2 again]

AI drafts. You edit. You send. And the final touches are yours.

Now it is your turn.

**Pause-and-try prompt:** Pause the video and type: "Write a polite follow-up email to a client who has not replied to my quote for five days. Keep it short and friendly." Ask it to "Make it warmer", then "Make it shorter". Then edit by hand: add your name, one personal detail, and fix anything untrue.

**Recap line:** AI writes the first version, and you make it true, clear and yours before it goes out.

**Bridge:** Writing is one half of the work, so next we look at the other half: turning long and messy material into something short and clear.

**Word count:** 1097 words (about 8.1 min at 135 wpm)

## m3-l4 | Job 3: Summarise long and messy things | target 9 min

**Learning objective:** The learner can turn long or messy material into short points or an action table, tell the chatbot to write "not stated" for missing information, and check the result against the original.

**Slides to prepare:**
1. Title: "Job 3: Summarise long and messy things".
2. Three cases: Long document (a few bullet points). Meeting notes (the action items). Messy notes (a table: task, owner, due date).
3. Example prompt: "Summarize this 3-page report into 3 key takeaways."
4. The sample notes (paste text, see the script).
5. The prompt: "Make a table with three columns: task, owner, due date. If something is missing, write not stated."
6. The expected table: Send venue quotes, Grace, Monday. Confirm 40 guests, Not stated, Not stated. Design the invitation, Brian, Before the 14th.
7. Check list: Is every row in the notes? Is anything left out? Are the numbers right?

**Script:**

Welcome back. Today is Job 3: summarise.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 1, title]

To summarise is to make something long shorter, while keeping the main points. A summary is not a copy. It is the part you need to know.

We all have long and messy things in our lives. A forty-page handout from a lecturer. A WhatsApp thread with ninety messages. Minutes of a meeting that nobody wrote down properly. A report from work that you must read before tomorrow morning.

You do not always have time to read every line. A chatbot can help you get from long and messy to short and clear. Let me show you three ways.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 2, three cases]

First, a long document. You can ask for it to be condensed into a few bullet points. A bullet point is one short line in a list.

Second, meeting notes. You can ask for the action items. An action item is a task that someone has to do.

Third, messy notes. You can ask for a table, with the task, the owner and the due date. The owner is the person responsible for the task.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 3, example prompt]

Here is a simple prompt for the first case: "Summarize this 3-page report into 3 key takeaways." A takeaway is the main thing to remember. Notice the number: three. It tells the chatbot how short you want it. That is the format part of the three-part request you learned in Module 2.

A university student might use this on a public article. A small business owner might use it on a long report. In both cases, the chatbot has something to work with, because you gave it the text.

But there is a warning. A summary can leave out something important. It can also change a number or a name. So we always check the summary against the original. Let me show you.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 4, the notes]

Here are some messy notes from a planning call. This is the try-it-now task. I will do it first.

[DEMO: Open a chatbot such as ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude. Paste exactly these notes: "Planning call, Friday. Grace will send venue quotes by Monday. We need to confirm 40 guests. Joseph says catering budget is 2 million. Brian to design the invitation, needs it before the 14th. Still not sure if the hall is free on the 20th."]

Read them with me. "Planning call, Friday. Grace will send venue quotes by Monday. We need to confirm 40 guests. Joseph says catering budget is 2 million. Brian to design the invitation, needs it before the 14th. Still not sure if the hall is free on the 20th."

It is a bit messy, is it not? The tasks are hidden inside sentences. Some have a person. Some do not. Some have a date. Some do not.

Now the request.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 5, the prompt]

[DEMO: Add the request and send. Type exactly: "Make a table with three columns: task, owner, due date. If something is missing, write not stated." A good result is a table with three columns and three rows: Send venue quotes, Grace, Monday. Confirm 40 guests, Not stated, Not stated. Design the invitation, Brian, Before the 14th.]

I type: "Make a table with three columns: task, owner, due date. If something is missing, write not stated."

And here is the table. [PAUSE]

[ON SCREEN: Slide 6, the expected table]

Let me read it. Send venue quotes. Owner: Grace. Due: Monday. Confirm 40 guests. Owner: not stated. Due: not stated. Design the invitation. Owner: Brian. Due: before the 14th.

Why did I add "If something is missing, write not stated"? Because of how chatbots work. A chatbot predicts what fits. If there is a gap, it may fill it with a guess. It might put Grace as the owner of the guest list, because her name is nearby. It might invent a due date. That would look neat, and it would be wrong.

When I tell it to write "not stated", it has a safe way to say "the notes do not say". That is much better than a confident guess.

Now the most important step. I check the table against the notes.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 7, check list]

[DEMO: Go through the table row by row, pointing to the matching words in the pasted notes. Tick each one.]

Row one. Grace, venue quotes, by Monday. Yes, it is in the notes. Row two. Confirm 40 guests. The notes say we need to, but not who does it or when. So "not stated" is correct. Row three. Brian, the invitation, before the 14th. Yes.

Now I ask a second question. Is anything left out? Look back at the notes. Joseph says the catering budget is 2 million. That is not a task, so it is not in the table. And we are not sure if the hall is free on the 20th. That is a question that someone needs to answer.

The table did not lose them by mistake. They are not tasks. But I do not want to forget them. So I ask a follow-up.

[DEMO: Type exactly: "What information in the notes is not in the table?" A good result lists the catering budget of 2 million and the open question about whether the hall is free on the 20th.]

I type: "What information in the notes is not in the table?" And it gives me the catering budget and the hall question. Now I have a table of tasks, and a short list of open points. I can send that to the team, after I check it once more.

This is what AI drafts, you decide looks like in this job. The chatbot sorted the messy notes. I made sure nothing was invented and nothing important was lost.

[PAUSE]

Let me add some practical points.

One. Always give the chatbot the text. Do not just ask it to summarise a document it has not seen. In Module 2 you learned that AI works better when you give it your own material.

Two. Tell it how short, and in what shape. "Three key takeaways." "A table with three columns." "Five bullet points."

Three. Check the numbers, the names and the dates. In our notes, the numbers were 40 guests, 2 million, the 14th and the 20th. A good habit is to find each one in the original.

Here is how that looks with the three-takeaways prompt. Say I paste a public article and ask for three key takeaways. I do not just copy them. I find the place in the article where each takeaway comes from. If I cannot find it, I do not use it. That takes two minutes, and it protects me from passing on something the chatbot made up.

Four. If it matters, read the original too. A summary is a shortcut for first understanding. It is not always a replacement for reading the important part yourself.

[PAUSE]

Now the privacy point, and it matters a lot here. Real meeting notes often have real names, phone numbers, salaries and private business decisions. Notes like that should not go into a public chatbot. The notes I used are sample notes. They are safe to paste. For your own work, remove names and private numbers first, or describe the problem instead of pasting the whole text. For a full contract, remove names and amounts, or use a tool your organisation has approved. A mobile money statement, a client list and a copy of someone's ID never go in. Module 5 explains this in detail.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 2 again]

So the job is simple. Take something long or messy. Tell the chatbot what shape you want. Ask for "not stated" where there are gaps. Then check it against the original.

Now it is your turn.

**Pause-and-try prompt:** Pause the video and paste the sample planning-call notes into your chatbot. Ask: "Make a table with three columns: task, owner, due date. If something is missing, write not stated." Then check every row against the notes.

**Learning objective:** The learner can ask a chatbot for a spreadsheet formula in plain words, understand its explanation, and test it on one row with a calculator before trusting it.

**Slides to prepare:**

**Script:**

Welcome back. This is the last job in the module: automate.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 1, title]

To automate is to let a tool do repeating work for you, so you do not do it by hand every time.

And you can do this without writing code. Code is the written instructions that a computer follows. Many people think that you must be a programmer to automate anything. With a chatbot, you can describe what you want in plain words. It can help close the gap between what you can imagine and what you can build on your own.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 2, three cases]

Here are three ways to use it.

First, spreadsheet formulas. A spreadsheet is a grid of rows and columns, such as Excel or Google Sheets. A formula is an instruction that a spreadsheet follows to calculate something. You can ask a chatbot for a formula in plain words.

Second, troubleshooting. When software is not behaving, you can ask for step-by-step help.

Third, small macros. A macro is a small set of steps that a spreadsheet can repeat for you, to save repetitive clicking and copying.

Today we spend our time on the first one, because it is the one you can try straight away.

Think of a baker in Entebbe who writes down her sales at the end of every month. She wants to know: did I sell more this month than last month, and by how much? She could work it out on a calculator for each month. Or she could ask a chatbot for a formula and let the spreadsheet do it every time.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 3, the prompt]

Let me show you the try-it-now task.

[DEMO: Have a spreadsheet open, Excel or Google Sheets, with the sample sales: column A months (Jan, Feb, Mar, Apr), column B sales in UGX starting in B2: 1,200,000; 1,380,000; 1,242,000; 1,490,400. Leave column C empty.]

Here is my spreadsheet. Column A has the months. Column B has the sales. These are sample numbers I made up for this lesson. They start in cell B2. A cell is one box in the grid.

I want a growth figure in column C. Growth means how much sales went up or down compared with last month, shown as a percentage.

Now I go to the chatbot. I do not need to know the formula. I describe it.

[DEMO: Open a chatbot such as ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude. Type exactly: "My monthly sales are in column B, starting at B2. Write a formula in C3 that shows growth compared with last month as a percentage, and explain it in simple words." A good result looks like =(B3-B2)/B2, with a plain explanation: take this month, subtract last month, divide by last month, and format the cell as a percentage.]

I type: "My monthly sales are in column B, starting at B2. Write a formula in C3 that shows growth compared with last month as a percentage, and explain it in simple words."

And here is the answer. [PAUSE]

It should look something like this: equals, open bracket, B3 minus B2, close bracket, divided by B2.

And it explains it. Take this month's sales. Subtract last month's. That gives the change. Divide the change by last month's sales. That gives the growth. Then format the cell as a percentage.

Notice that I asked for the explanation. That matters. If I do not understand the formula, I cannot check it. And we are going to check it.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 4, sample table]

[DEMO: Click on C3, type or paste the formula, press Enter. Format column C as a percentage. Fill the formula down to C4 and C5.]

I paste it into C3. I format the column as a percentage. And it shows fifteen percent.

[PAUSE]

Now, do I believe it? Not yet.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 5, the worked check]

Sounding right is not the same as being right. The formula looks sensible. The explanation sounds sensible. But I have not tested it. So I test it on one row, with a calculator.

[DEMO: Open a calculator app or hold up a handheld calculator, and work through it on screen.]

February sales are 1,380,000. January sales are 1,200,000. One million three hundred and eighty thousand minus one million two hundred thousand is 180,000. Now 180,000 divided by 1,200,000 is 0.15. As a percentage, that is 15 percent.

The spreadsheet said 15 percent. The calculator says 15 percent. They match.

Good. Now I trust this formula for this job. And I trust it because I tested it, not because the chatbot sounded sure.

If you want to be extra careful, check a second row too. March fell compared with February. The formula should show a negative number, which means sales went down. Let me look. Yes, it shows a drop of ten percent. That makes sense, because 1,242,000 is lower than 1,380,000.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 6]

Now, one more question that a careful person asks. What if last month could be zero? Imagine a new product that sold nothing in its first month. The formula would try to divide by zero. A spreadsheet cannot do that, and it will show an error.

So I go back to the chatbot. I do not start again. I refine.

[DEMO: Type exactly: "Change the formula so it does not show an error if last month's sales are zero." A good result is a version that checks for zero first, for example one that uses an IF test and shows a message or a blank when the earlier month is zero. Ask the chatbot to explain it in simple words, and then test it on the row that has a normal value to see that the answer is still 15 percent.]

I type: "Change the formula so it does not show an error if last month's sales are zero."

And it gives me a new version, with an extra check at the start. I read the explanation. I test it again on the normal row, to make sure it still gives me 15 percent. It does.

[PAUSE]

Let me say something about learning. It is tempting to let the chatbot do everything and learn nothing. But that makes you dependent. Use it to speed up while you learn the skill, not to avoid learning it.

How? Ask for the explanation, every time. Read it. Next time, try to write a similar formula yourself, and ask the chatbot to check it. Over a few weeks, you will need it less for the easy ones.

The same goes for troubleshooting. If a program gives an error message, you can describe what happened and what you see, and ask for step-by-step help. Do the steps one at a time. Check that each one works before moving on.

And for macros, which are the small repeating jobs: start small, test on a copy, and never run something you do not understand on your only copy of important work.

[PAUSE]

Now the privacy point, once more. When you ask for a formula, you do not need to paste your real data. Describe the columns, as I did. Use made-up numbers to test. Do not paste a customer list, a mobile money statement, or anyone's personal details into a public chatbot. Describe the problem, not the person. We cover this fully in Module 5.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 8, Module 3 wrap-up]

Let me pull the whole module together. Four jobs. Brainstorm, to beat the blank page. Draft, for a first version that you make your own. Summarise, to turn long and messy into short and clear. Automate, to do repetitive work without writing code.

And one rule for all of them. AI drafts, you decide. Before you use anything, ask three questions. Is it true? Is it right for my reader? Is it safe to share?
