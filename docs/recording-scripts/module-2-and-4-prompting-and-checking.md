## m2-l2 | Context, task, format: the three-part prompt | target 9 min

**Learning objective:** By the end of this video you can write a request with three parts, context, task and format, and see how much better the answer becomes.

**Slides to prepare:**
1. Title: "Context, task, format".
2. Weak versus strong: "Write an email." on one side, the bakery prompt on the other.
3. Three boxes: Context (who you are and the situation), Task (what you want it to do), Format (length, tone, shape).
4. The bakery prompt split into three colours: context, task, format.
5. A boda boda example with the three parts labelled.
6. A student example with the three parts labelled.
7. "More useful detail in, more useful answer out."
8. Try it now: "Write an email." then your own version.

**Script:**

Let me start with a short story.

Imagine you walk into a shop in Entebbe, and you say to the shopkeeper, "Give me something." What will happen? The shopkeeper will ask you ten questions. What do you want? For whom? How much do you have?

Now imagine you say, "I need a gift for my sister's birthday on Saturday. She likes books. I can spend about UGX 30,000." Now the shopkeeper knows exactly what to do.

A chatbot is the same. It cannot read your mind. It only has the words you type. The more useful detail you give, the more useful the answer you get. That is the idea for this whole video.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 2, weak versus strong]

Here is a weak request.

[ON SCREEN: "Write an email."]

"Write an email." That is all. Who is it to? What is it about? How long? Is it friendly or formal? The chatbot has to guess every one of those. So it gives you something general, and probably not what you need.

Now look at a strong request.

[ON SCREEN: The bakery prompt in full]

"I run a small bakery in Entebbe. Write a short, friendly email to a customer confirming their cake order for Saturday, UGX 120,000, pickup at 10am. Keep it under 100 words."

Notice how different that is. It says who you are. It says what you want. It says how you want it. And the answer will be far closer to something you can send.

[PAUSE]

A good request has three parts. Let us take them one at a time.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 3, the three boxes]

The first part is context. Context means who you are and the situation. It is the background the chatbot needs. In the bakery example, the context is: "I run a small bakery in Entebbe." Now it knows you are a business owner, and it knows the place. It will write like a baker would, not like a bank.

The second part is the task. The task is what you want it to do. In the bakery example: "Write a short, friendly email to a customer confirming their cake order." Use a clear action word. Write, explain, list, compare, summarise, translate. One clear task is better than five muddled ones.

The third part is the format. Format is how you want the answer to look. Length, tone and shape. In the bakery example: "Keep it under 100 words." You could also say "friendly", or "in three bullet points", or "as a short message I can send on WhatsApp". Format saves you editing time, because the answer arrives in a shape you can use.

Context, task, format. Say it with me. Context, task, format.

[PAUSE]

Let us look at the bakery prompt again, this time with the parts marked.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 4, the bakery prompt in three colours]

"I run a small bakery in Entebbe." That is the context. "Write a short, friendly email to a customer confirming their cake order for Saturday, UGX 120,000, pickup at 10am." That is the task, and it carries the details the customer needs. "Keep it under 100 words." That is the format.

Now I will show you this live, so you can see the difference with your own eyes.

[DEMO: Open your chatbot. Type exactly: Write an email.]

[DEMO: Send it. Read the answer aloud, and point out how general it is.]

See that? It asks me who the email is for, or it writes something vague. It had to guess.

Now the strong version.

[DEMO: Start a new chat. Type exactly: I run a small bakery in Entebbe. Write a short, friendly email to a customer confirming their cake order for Saturday, UGX 120,000, pickup at 10am. Keep it under 100 words.]

[DEMO: Send it. Read the answer aloud. Point out the order, the price, the pickup time and the length.]

Much better. The date is there. The amount is there. The pickup time is there. And it is short. Same chatbot, same minute. The only difference is the words I typed.

[PAUSE]

Now let me give you two more examples, so you can see this works for anyone.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 5, the boda boda example]

First, a boda boda rider. Say his name is Moses. He wants to tell regular customers that he now takes bookings by phone for the airport run. A weak prompt would be: "Write a message." A strong prompt would be this.

"I am a boda boda rider in Entebbe with many regular customers. Write a short message I can send on WhatsApp telling them I now take bookings for airport trips. Keep it friendly, under 60 words, and end with a request to call me."

The context is the rider with regular customers. The task is the message about airport bookings. The format is short, friendly, under 60 words, ending with a request to call.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 6, the student example]

Second, a university student. Say her name is Nakato. She has a presentation on Monday. A weak prompt would be: "Help me with my presentation." A strong prompt would be this.

"I am a second-year student, and I have to give a five-minute presentation on saving money. Give me an outline of five points, each with one sentence, in simple English."

The context is a second-year student with a five-minute talk. The task is an outline. The format is five points, one sentence each, simple English.

[PAUSE]

Let me answer a question you may be asking. Does this mean I must always write long prompts? No. It means you should include the detail that matters. Sometimes that is one sentence. Sometimes it is four. If you are unsure, ask yourself three questions before you press send. Does it know who I am and what the situation is? Does it know what I want it to do? Does it know how I want the answer to look?

If you can say yes to all three, you are ready.

Also, notice something about the details. In the bakery example, I used UGX 120,000 and a pickup time. Those details came from me, not from the chatbot. The chatbot does not know your prices or your customers. You do. So you give it the real facts, and it helps you with the words.

Here is a small tip about order. You can put the three parts in any order, as long as all three are there. Many people start with context, then task, then format, because it feels natural, like telling a story. Use whichever order feels easiest.

Another tip. If the answer misses the mark, look at your three parts. Which one was missing or too thin? Usually it is the context or the format. Fix that part, and try again. Next video, you will see an even faster way to improve an answer.

One more thing. Do not put private details in. For the bakery, I did not type the customer's name or phone number. I only gave the order details. You can add the name yourself when you edit. We will say more about this in a later video.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 7, "More useful detail in, more useful answer out."]

So let us pull it together. A good request has three parts. Context, who you are and the situation. Task, what you want it to do. Format, how you want it, such as length, tone and shape. More useful detail in, more useful answer out.

[PAUSE]

Now it is your turn. I want you to try this yourself, and I want you to compare.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 8, the try-it task]

**Pause-and-try prompt:** Type "Write an email." and send it. Then rewrite the request using your real situation, a clear task and a format, and send that too. Put the two answers side by side and compare them.

**Recap line:** Give it context, task and format, and the answer you get back is far more useful.

**Bridge:** Even a strong prompt will sometimes give an answer that is not quite right, so in the next video you will learn to refine it instead of starting again.

## m2-l3 | Not quite right? Refine, don't restart | target 6 min

**Learning objective:** By the end of this video you can improve an answer with short follow-up instructions instead of starting again.

**Slides to prepare:**
1. Title: "Not quite right? Refine, don't restart".
2. "Starting over throws away what was already good."
3. The refinement phrases: "Make it shorter." "Make it warmer." "Explain that in simpler words." "Use a more formal tone." "Give me three versions." "Add an example."
4. A before-and-after of a thank-you message to a customer.
5. Try it now: three refinements.

**Script:**

You wrote a good prompt. You sent it. And the answer is nearly right, but not quite. What do you do?

Many beginners do the same thing. They close the chat, start a new one, and type the whole request again. Please do not do that.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 2, "Starting over throws away what was already good."]

Think of a tailor in Entebbe. A customer comes to collect a shirt. The sleeves are a little long. Does the customer say, "Throw it away, and start again from the cloth"? Of course not. They say, "Please shorten the sleeves." The tailor adjusts the shirt. The collar, the buttons and the stitching all stay. Only the sleeves change.

That is how you should treat a chatbot answer. When it is not quite right, you do not start again. You give a follow-up instruction. This is called refining. To refine means to improve an answer with follow-up instructions.

[PAUSE]

Why does this work? Because the chatbot remembers what you have said in the same conversation. So when you type "Make it shorter", it knows what "it" means. It looks at the answer above and changes it. You do not have to repeat your context, your task and your format. They are already there.

Let me show you a few phrases you can use. You do not have to memorise them. Just keep them nearby.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 3, the six phrases, appearing one at a time]

"Make it shorter." Use this when the answer is too long for a WhatsApp message or a notice.

"Make it warmer." Use this when the answer sounds cold or stiff, like a message to a loyal customer.

"Explain that in simpler words." Use this when you did not understand something. This one is very useful for students.

"Use a more formal tone." Use this when the message is for a bank, a school office or a government office.

"Give me three versions." Use this when you want choices, so that you can pick the one that sounds most like you.

"Add an example." Use this when an idea feels abstract.

Six short instructions, and notice that none of them is long or clever. Short and plain works best.

[PAUSE]

Now let me show you how it works. I will ask for a short thank-you message to a customer, and then I will refine it step by step.

[DEMO: Open your chatbot. Type exactly: Write a short thank-you message to a customer who bought a dress from my tailoring shop in Entebbe.]

[DEMO: Send it. Read the answer aloud.]

It is fine. But maybe it is a little stiff. So I will not start again. I will type one thing.

[DEMO: Type exactly: Make it warmer.]

[DEMO: Send it. Read the new version aloud, and point out what changed.]

Better. It sounds more like a friendly shop owner. But it is a bit long for a WhatsApp message. So again, one instruction.

[DEMO: Type exactly: Make it shorter.]

[DEMO: Send it. Read the answer aloud.]

Now it is warm and short. And I did not lose anything. Now I want choices.

[DEMO: Type exactly: Give me three versions.]

[DEMO: Send it. Read the three versions aloud.]

I now have three options. I will pick the one I like best. But I am not finished. This last step is the most important one.

[PAUSE]

I will edit it by hand. I will add the customer's name, because I did not type it into the chatbot. I will add one personal detail, such as the day the dress will be ready, and I will fix anything that is not true. The chatbot does not know my shop hours or my prices. It may even have guessed something. So I read it carefully before I send it.

The chatbot gave me a draft. The final touches are mine.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 4, before-and-after]

Let me point out a few things you can do when you refine.

You can refine more than once. There is no limit to how many follow-ups you can give in a session. Two or three small changes usually get you there.

You can be specific. Instead of "Make it better", say how. "Make it shorter" tells it more than "make it better". "Make it warmer and add one sentence thanking them for choosing a local shop" tells it even more.

You can undo by asking. If you do not like a change, type something like "Go back to the first version." Often it can do that. Or you can say, "Keep the first paragraph, and change only the last one."

And sometimes you really do need to start fresh. If the chatbot has misunderstood your whole task, or the conversation has become long and confused, a new chat is fine. But that is the exception. Most of the time, refine.

[PAUSE]

Before we try it, one reminder. Refining makes an answer sound better. It does not make it true. If the message includes a price, a date or a fact, you are still the one who checks it. We will spend a whole module on that soon.

So let us review. When an answer is not quite right, do not start again. Give a follow-up instruction. Make it shorter. Make it warmer. Explain it in simpler words. Use a more formal tone. Give me three versions. Add an example. And then edit by hand.

[PAUSE]

Now it is your turn.

**Pause-and-try prompt:** Ask your chatbot for a short thank-you message to a customer. Then ask it to make the message warmer, then shorter, then to give you three versions. Pick your favourite and edit it by hand.

**Recap line:** When the answer is nearly right, refine it with a short follow-up instead of starting again.

## m2-l4 | Give it your own text to work from | target 7 min

**Learning objective:** By the end of this video you can paste your own text into a chatbot and tell it what to do with it, while keeping private details out.

**Slides to prepare:**
1. Title: "Give it your own text to work from".
2. "AI does much better when you give it the material."
3. Three things you can paste: a draft, a set of notes, a description. Each with a one-line instruction.
4. "Ask it to say when it does not know."
5. "What never goes in": passwords and PINs including mobile money PINs, ID numbers, other people's private details, confidential work information.
6. "Describe the problem, not the person." with a before and after.
7. Try it now: three rough sentences.

**Script:**

So far you have asked the chatbot to write things from nothing. Now I want to show you something that often works much better.

Give it your own material to work from.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 2]

Think about a market stall in Entebbe. Imagine you ask a helper to "sell something". They will not know what to do. But if you give them the list of what you stock and the prices, they can start at once.

A chatbot is like that helper. When it has your material in front of it, it does not have to guess. It works from what you gave it. And the result is usually closer to what you meant.

[PAUSE]

What can you give it? Three common things.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 3, three things]

One. A draft. This is something you wrote yourself, and you want it improved. A paragraph for a school assignment, a short bio for your CV, a message to a customer.

Two. A set of notes. These might be rough lines from a meeting, or from a class, or from a phone call. They are untidy, and you want them organised.

Three. A description. You describe a situation in your own words, and you ask for help with it.

In each case, you do two things. You paste in the text, and you say what to do with it.

Here is the pattern. First say the instruction. Then paste the text. For example: "Improve this for a friendly newsletter, keeping the meaning." And then the text below it.

That is the same three-part idea you learnt before. The context and the material are the text you pasted. The task is what you want done. The format is how you want it to look.

Let me show you.

[DEMO: Open your chatbot. Type exactly the following, with the three rough sentences on new lines.]

[DEMO: Type: Improve this for a friendly newsletter, keeping the meaning.
Today I went to the market early. The tomatoes were cheap but the onions were expensive. I met my old teacher there and we talked a long time.]

[DEMO: Send it. Read the answer aloud.]

Now compare. Look at my three sentences and look at the answer. What did it keep? It kept the market, the tomatoes, the onions and the old teacher. What did it add? Maybe a friendly opening, or some words that I did not say. Perhaps a small detail that never happened.

That last point matters. When you give the chatbot your text, always read the result against your original. Check that it kept your meaning, and check that it did not add anything untrue.

[PAUSE]

There is another very useful thing you can do when you give it your text. You can ask it to say when it does not know.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 4, "Ask it to say when it does not know."]

Why is this needed? Because a chatbot will usually try to answer, even when the information is not there. So you can say: "Use only the text I gave you. If something is not in the text, write not stated."

Let me show you with a few notes.

[DEMO: Type exactly: Use only the notes below. Write the date, the place and the cost in a list. If something is not in the notes, write "not stated".
Wedding meeting on Friday. Cost for the tent is UGX 600,000. We still do not know if the garden is free.]

[DEMO: Send it. Read the answer aloud. Point out that the date and place show "not stated" or the cost is listed correctly.]

See how it handled the missing information. It did not invent a venue. It said "not stated", because I told it to. That is a simple way to cut down on made-up answers. It does not remove them, and I will explain why in a later video. But it helps.

[PAUSE]

Now we come to the most important part of this video. Be careful what you paste.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 5, "What never goes in"]

Remember what we said when we set up your tool. Free tools may use what you type. So nothing private, confidential or personal should go into a public tool. A public tool is a chatbot that anyone can use.

What does that include? Passwords and PINs, including your mobile money PIN. National ID and passport numbers. Bank and card details. Other people's private details. And confidential information from your work or your business.

So before you paste anything, do three small things.

First, read it through. Look for names, phone numbers, ID numbers, addresses and amounts that identify a person.

Second, remove them. Delete them, or replace them with something general, like "the customer" or "Client A".

Third, ask yourself: if this text were read out in a public place, would it matter? If the answer is yes, do not paste it.

[PAUSE]

There is a simple saying that helps here. Describe the problem, not the person.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 6, before and after]

Let me show you what I mean. Say a customer has sent a complaint to your shop. It is tempting to paste the whole message, with their name and their phone number. Do not do that.

Instead, describe the problem. "A customer says their delivery was two days late. Write a polite reply." That gives the chatbot everything it needs, and it protects the customer. You can add the name yourself when you edit.

Another example. You want help with a message to a tenant about late rent. Do not paste the tenant's name, the house number and the amount. Write: "A tenant is a week late with rent. Write a firm but kind message asking them to pay by Friday." Then add the details yourself after.

[PAUSE]

Let me gather it up. AI does much better when you give it your own material. Paste in a draft, notes or a description, and say what to do with it. Ask it to say when it does not know. Remove names, numbers and private details first. And describe the problem, not the person.

Now it is your turn. This one uses your own words, so it is fun.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 7, the try-it task]

**Pause-and-try prompt:** Write three rough sentences about your day, with no names or private details. Paste them and ask: "Improve this for a friendly newsletter, keeping the meaning." Then compare, and note what it kept and what it added.

**Recap line:** Give the chatbot your own text, tell it what to do, and keep every private detail out.

## m4-l1 | Why AI sounds sure and is wrong | target 7 min

**Learning objective:** By the end of this video you can explain what a hallucination is, why it happens, and why a made-up answer can look more convincing than a true one.

**Slides to prepare:**
1. Title: "Why AI sounds sure and is wrong".
2. "AI can sound completely sure and be completely wrong."
3. "Hallucination: when AI states something false as if it were true." With three words under it: facts, quotes, sources, numbers.
4. "It predicts what sounds likely. It does not check what is true."
5. The phone keyboard example: "Good" then "morning".
6. "A made-up answer can look more convincing than a true one."
7. Try it now: The Golden Boda of Nansana.

**Script:**

Welcome to Module 4. You now know how to ask AI well. This module is about the other side. What happens when the answer is wrong.

I want to start with a simple, honest statement.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 2]

AI can sound completely sure and be completely wrong.

I will say that again, because it is the most important sentence in this module. AI can sound completely sure, and be completely wrong.

[PAUSE]

Imagine a man at a bus stop in Kampala. Someone asks him, "Which bus goes to Entebbe?" He does not know. But he does not want to look foolish. So he points confidently and says, "That one, with the blue stripe."

He is calm. He is clear. He sounds sure. And he may be completely wrong. You only find out when you are on the wrong bus.

A chatbot can behave a little like that man. It does not mean to mislead you. But it can give you an answer in a calm, confident voice, with nice words and good grammar, and the answer can be false.

[PAUSE]

There is a name for this. It is called a hallucination.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 3, the definition]

A hallucination is when AI states something false as if it were true.

What kind of things can it make up? Facts. Quotes. Sources. And numbers. It may give you the name of a book that does not exist. It may give you a quote that nobody said. It may give you a web link that goes nowhere. It may give you a figure that looks exact, but is invented.

Notice the words "as if it were true". The chatbot does not wave a flag and say "I am guessing here". It says it in the same tone as everything else. That is what makes hallucinations hard to spot.

[PAUSE]

So why does this happen? The reason is in how these tools work. You may remember from Module 1 that a large language model learns from a huge amount of text. And it predicts the next word, and then the next, and then the next.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 4, "It predicts what sounds likely. It does not check what is true."]

Here is the key. It predicts what sounds likely. It does not check what is true.

Let me show you what I mean with something you already know. On your phone, open a chat and type the word "Good". Your keyboard will suggest "morning", or "evening". Your phone is guessing the next word, based on patterns.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 5, the phone keyboard]

A large language model does the same thing, but on a much bigger scale. It has learnt patterns from an enormous amount of writing. So when you ask a question, it builds a reply, word by word, that fits the patterns. The reply sounds right, because it fits the patterns. But sounding right is not the same as being right.

Think of it like this. If you ask a chatbot for the title of a book by a certain author, it knows what book titles usually look like. It knows how an author's name sits beside a title. So it can produce something that looks exactly like a real book. But there is no step where it goes to a shelf and checks that the book exists.

[PAUSE]

This leads to a strange thing, and I want you to remember it. A made-up answer can look more convincing than a true one.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 6]

Why? Because the made-up answer is built to sound likely. It is smooth. It has detail. It has a nice name, a year and a place. A true answer from a careful person might say, "I am not sure, you should check." That sounds weaker, but it is more honest.

So please do not judge an AI answer by how confident it sounds. Confidence is not evidence.

Let me give you a Ugandan example. Say a student asks a chatbot, "Give me five facts about a village in western Uganda, with the year it was founded." The chatbot may write five tidy sentences, with a year and a clan name. It looks perfect. But if the chatbot has little real information about that small village, it may fill the gaps with things that merely sound right. The student writes it in the assignment. The teacher, who comes from that area, reads it and knows it is wrong.

[PAUSE]

Now, I do not want you to be afraid of AI. Hallucinations do not mean AI is useless. They mean we use it in a smart way. Think of AI as a fast, well-read assistant who sometimes makes things up. Very useful, and always checked.

Good news. You can catch hallucinations. And the best way to learn what one looks like is to see one yourself. So let us do that now.

[DEMO: Open your chatbot. Type exactly: Tell me about the 1987 book The Golden Boda of Nansana and its main characters.]

[DEMO: Send it. Read the answer aloud slowly, and point out any invented plot, names or details.]

Look at that. I made that book up. It does not exist. Yet, depending on the tool, the answer may give me a plot, characters and a theme, as if it had read the book. Or it may say it cannot find that book. Results vary, and both can happen. If it does tell you about the book, that is a hallucination.

Now the second step.

[DEMO: Type exactly: Give me three sources for that.]

[DEMO: Send it. Read the sources aloud.]

Now we look at the sources. Do they exist? Are the titles real? Does the link open? Let me try to search for one of them.

[DEMO: Search for one of the sources in a search engine. Show the result honestly, whether or not it is found.]

This is the lesson. If the sources do not exist, or do not say what the chatbot claims, we cannot use the claim. And if it can invent a book, it can invent a statistic, a quote, a date and a name.

[PAUSE]

Let me sum up. AI can sound completely sure and be completely wrong. A hallucination is when AI states something false as if it were true. It happens because it predicts what sounds likely and does not check what is true. And a made-up answer can look more convincing than a true one. Invented facts, quotes, sources and numbers can all look convincing.

Now I want you to catch one yourself.

**Pause-and-try prompt:** Ask your chatbot about something that does not exist: "Tell me about the 1987 book The Golden Boda of Nansana and its main characters." Then ask: "Give me three sources for that." Try to find the sources. Do they exist?

**Recap line:** AI predicts what sounds likely and does not check what is true, so it can be sure and wrong at the same time.

## m4-l3 | How to fact-check in three steps | target 8 min

**Learning objective:** By the end of this video you can fact-check an AI answer in three steps and know when to check more carefully.

**Slides to prepare:**
1. Title: "How to fact-check in three steps".
2. "Fact-check: verify a claim with a trusted source outside the chatbot."
3. Step 1: Mark the claims that matter. Names, numbers, dates, quotes, sources and laws.
4. Step 2: Check them outside the chatbot. An official website, a textbook, a trusted news source, or an expert.
5. Step 3: Ask for sources, then open them. "If the link does not exist or does not say that, do not use the claim."
6. "The riskier the topic, the more you check: health, law, money and safety."
7. "You can reduce hallucinations. You cannot remove them." with the three ways: give it the text, ask it to say when it does not know, ask for sources.
8. Try it now: use the three steps on one real answer.

**Script:**

In the last two videos, you learnt that AI can be sure and wrong, and that people are responsible for what they use. Now the question is, what do you actually do about it?

The answer is a simple habit. It is called fact-checking. To fact-check means to verify a claim with a trusted source outside the chatbot.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 2]

Look at the words "outside the chatbot". That is the key. Asking the same chatbot, "Are you sure?" is not a check. It may just say, "Yes, I am sure", in the same confident voice. You need a second source, one that is not the chatbot.

The method has three steps. It takes a few minutes, and with practice, less.

[PAUSE]

Step one. Mark the claims that matter.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 3, step one]

You do not need to check every word. If you did, you would never finish. Instead, read the answer and mark the claims that matter. A claim is a statement that says something is true.

What kinds of claims should you mark? Names, numbers, dates, quotes, sources and laws. These are the things that are easy to invent and costly to get wrong.

Let me show you. Say a student asks a chatbot about the history of mobile money in East Africa for an assignment. The answer comes back with a paragraph. I read it, and I underline three things. A year when a service started. A figure about how many people use it. And a name of a report. Those are my claims to check. The rest of the paragraph, the general explanation of how mobile money works, I read, and I use my own knowledge to judge it.

[DEMO: Ask your chatbot: Write a short paragraph about how mobile money has helped small traders in Uganda. Include one statistic and one source.]

[DEMO: Read the answer aloud. Underline or highlight each number, date, name and source in the answer.]

Look at what I have highlighted. A number. A named source. These are the claims I will check. I am not going to trust them yet.

[PAUSE]

Step two. Check them outside the chatbot.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 4, step two]

Where do you check? Four places work well. An official website. A textbook. A trusted news source. Or an expert.

An official website means the website of the body that would know. For a law, the government's own site. For a school rule, the school or the ministry. For a mobile money tariff, the company's own page or the agent's printed list. For a price at a shop, the shop itself.

A textbook is good for settled facts, such as definitions, history and science.

A trusted news source is good for recent events. Choose one you know to be careful, and see whether more than one reports it.

An expert is a person who knows. A doctor, a pharmacist, a lawyer, a teacher, an accountant. Remember that for the most serious questions, a person is the best check of all.

[DEMO: Take the highlighted number from the paragraph. Open a search engine or an official website and search for it. Show honestly whether you can find it.]

Let me check this one. I search for the figure on an official site. If I find the same figure, with the same year, good. If I find a different one, I use the real one. If I find nothing, I do not use the claim.

[PAUSE]

Step three. Ask for sources, then open them.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 5, step three]

You can ask the chatbot for its sources. Type: "Give me the source for each of these claims." This is useful, because it gives you a starting point to check.

But here is the catch, and you saw it in the first video with the Golden Boda. The sources can be invented too. So you must open them. Click the link. Find the document. Look for the claim inside it.

[DEMO: Type exactly: Give me the source for each of these claims.]

[DEMO: Read the sources. Try to open one link or search for one title. Show the result honestly.]

And here is the rule. If the link does not exist, or does not say that, do not use the claim.

Say that with me. If the link does not exist, or does not say that, do not use the claim.

That includes a link that opens but says something different. A real page is not enough. It has to support the claim.

[PAUSE]

Mark the claims that matter. Check them outside the chatbot. Ask for sources, then open them. Three steps.

Now, one more idea. Not everything needs the same effort.

[ON SCREEN: Slide 6, "The riskier the topic, the more you check."]

The riskier the topic, the more you check. Which topics are the riskiest? Health, law, money and safety.

Think about it. If a chatbot suggests a name for your shop, and the name is odd, nothing bad happens. You pick another. But if it tells you the dose of a medicine for a child, a mistake could harm someone. If it tells you what a law says, a mistake could land you in trouble. If it gives you advice about money, a mistake could cost you your savings. And if it gives you a safety instruction, a mistake could hurt someone.

For those four topics, treat the chatbot as a place to start, not a place to finish. Ask a doctor or a pharmacist. Read the law from an official source. Check money questions with a trusted source or an expert. And do not guess when safety is at stake.

[PAUSE]

Now, a question people often ask. Can I stop AI from hallucinating at all?

[ON SCREEN: Slide 7]

The honest answer is, you can reduce it, but you cannot remove it. There are three things that help, and you have already learnt all three.

First, give it the text to work from. You practised this in Module 2. When it has your material, it has less need to invent.

Second, ask it to say when it does not know. Tell it to write "not stated" or to say it is not sure.

Third, ask it for sources, and then open them.

These make hallucinations less likely. But they do not make them impossible. So the fact-check stays.

One more note for Luganda speakers. Chatbots often do worse in Luganda, because there is less data in that language. If you use a chatbot to translate or write in Luganda, ask a fluent speaker to check anything important.

[PAUSE]

Let me put the whole method together. Mark the claims that matter. Check them outside the chatbot. Ask for sources, then open them. And check more when the topic is health, law, money or safety.

Now it is your turn to try it.

**Pause-and-try prompt:** Ask your chatbot a factual question about something you can check, such as a short paragraph with a number and a source. Mark the claims that matter, check at least one outside the chatbot, then open its source and see whether it says what the chatbot claims.

**Recap line:** Mark the claims that matter, check them outside the chatbot, and open the sources, because if the link does not exist or does not say that, you do not use the claim.
